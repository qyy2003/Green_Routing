Why long context helps Timestral and not our models

Old weeks are useful for one thing: a better estimate of each link's level (its daily volume) and of its daily shape. Timestral gets that from them; blanking its weeks 5 to 24 costs it 0.75 (I2) and 1.44 points (Switch LAN). Our models also read the old weeks and fit the training period better with them, but what they learn there does not carry over to later months. Five years of I2 history and six times more series remove the damage, but no gain appears. Preprocessing is not the gap, and fine-tuning Timestral on our data changes nothing. What Timestral has and we cannot train from one network is a prior for reading long, noisy history.

What long context is for

Split each day-ahead error in two. The volume error is how far the forecast day's total traffic is from the real total. The shape error is the hourly error left after scaling each forecast day to the true total. Long context should help with both: many weeks give a steadier estimate of a link's level and of its usual day.

With one week of history our trained models estimate the daily volume better than Timestral, because they have learned how these links usually behave. From there Timestral keeps improving as it sees more weeks, in volume and in shape. Our models stop at about 4 weeks, and at 24 weeks their shape error grows.

Our models read the old weeks but learn the wrong lessons

To see whether a model uses weeks 5 to 24, we blank them at test time and forecast again. If its forecasts do not move, it ignores them. Our 24-week models do not ignore them. When the old weeks are blanked their forecasts move by 3 to 8 % of the traffic, about as much as Timestral's (4 to 7 %), and their error rises by 0.6 to 2.5 points on I2.

The question is whether what they read there generalises. We score each model on three periods: the last 13 weeks of its own training data, the validation block, and the test block. Timestral never trained on any of them, so its scores show how hard each period is. On I2 the 24-week model beats the 4-week model on its own training days by 0.64 points and loses to it later. That is over-fitting: the long input lets the model memorise how this network behaved in the training months. On Switch LAN the effect is smaller and the 24-week model keeps a slight edge.

Is it the amount of data?

Partly. To test it we downloaded Internet2's hourly history back to the start of its current core network: 2021-05 to 2026-11, 5.1 years of training data instead of 1.6 for the same 113 links. We also took every customer port's traffic in and out (567 door series) as extra training series, six times more series in all. I2 keeps 5-minute data for only about a year, so this test runs on hourly input. Switch LAN's 5-minute export starts on 2023-12-29, and we already use all of it. Then we trained the same models with 6 months to 5 years of training targets.

The 24-week penalty falls fast as data grows, from 2.9 points with 6 months to 0.45 with 2 years, then slowly to 0.2 with 5 years and the doors. So data is what the long input was missing. But the curve flattens at the 4-week model's level, about 18.3 %, and the 4-week models do not improve with more data at all. Timestral sits at 16.2 %. Data from 2021 to 2023 shows no sign of closing that gap.

Fine-tuning Timestral

If our data held something Timestral does not know, fine-tuning on it should help. It does not. Its training loss on our windows (pinball, scaled by the context mean) stays flat at about 0.10 from the first epoch, and validation barely moves; on Switch LAN at the higher rate the best checkpoint is the original model. This is the same answer as fine-tuning on 1.6 years of I2 in the earlier report (−0.16, not significant).

The four ladder models on hourly input with our preprocessing and the history used before the new download (I2 from 2024-11, Switch LAN from 2024-01), one budget each (100,000 windows per epoch, at most 12 epochs). The small models are not hurt by long context, and on I2 the GRU and the TCN gain from it: 0.43 points (significant) and 0.64 (not significant) from 4 to 24 weeks. PatchTST, whose output layer has millions of weights, gets 1.16 (I2) and 0.76 points (Switch LAN) worse, as over-fitting predicts. What this means

  1. The limit is the estimator, not the input. Long history carries a modest, real signal about level and shape (0.75 to 1.44 points for Timestral). A model trained from scratch on one network learns to read it, but mostly learns how the network behaved in its training months.
  2. Data helps until the damage is gone, then stops. Five years of I2 and six times more series bring the 24-week model up to the 4-week model, not past it. Reaching Timestral's prior would take many networks' worth of varied series, the kind of corpus it was pretrained on.
  3. For production: use Timestral with 24 weeks of hourly input for day and week forecasts. For a trained model, use 4 weeks of context with our preprocessing. Fine-tuning Timestral on these networks is not worth the compute.

  • The I2 download covers the current core network only (from 2021-05); the older network (2016–2022) has different routers and links and was not used.
