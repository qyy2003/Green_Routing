Green Routing · carbon accounting with forecast traffic

SWITCHlan forecast carbon bills

Can SWITCH tell each customer and each peering partner, a day in advance, how much CO₂ its traffic will be billed? I joined the carbon accounting to the traffic forecasts and replayed the year benchmark's {{days}}-day test window ({{window_text}}), hour by hour. It works. The weak link is the grid-intensity forecast, not the traffic forecast.

Built {{built}} by recipes/carbon from the repository's data and public sources. Location-based, combustion-only (Scope 2) emissions throughout. A screening study, not an assured inventory.

In brief
  1. Over the window, SWITCHlan's network equipment drew {{kw_total}} kW at the sites (access {{kw_acc}} kW, backbone {{kw_bb}} kW) and emitted {{t_total}} t CO₂. Spring and summer grid electricity in Switzerland is very clean, about {{I_mean}} g/kWh.
  2. With the real grid factor, a day-ahead bill from the best traffic model ({{best_model}}) misses the settled daily bill by {{e_best_a}} WAPE, against {{e_pers_a}} for persistence.
  3. With a day-ahead grid forecast ({{grid_rule}}), the miss grows to {{e_best_f}}. The grid forecast alone, with perfect traffic, causes {{e_grid}}. Better grid forecasts are worth more than better traffic models.
  4. Sending flexible traffic in the hour the forecast marks as greenest cuts its billed g/GB from {{g_avg}} to {{g_pick}}. That captures {{captured}} of what hindsight would allow. The network's physical emissions barely move: an extra GB costs about {{marg_hi}} g, {{oom}} orders of magnitude below the billed rate.

1 · How the pieces fit

Every hour, each party's bill has two parts. The access part pays for equipment reserved for that party: its access router, or its share of a PoP router's customer-facing ports. It is split by allocated bandwidth. The backbone part pays for shared transport: trunk-facing router capacity and the whole optical layer. It is split by the bytes the party actually sends and receives. Both parts are energy times the hourly grid factor of the site's zone.

3 · Design choices

Each choice below names the alternative I rejected and the evidence that decided it.

Energy is static; do not forecast it from traffic

Router power is treated as constant per device, measured where possible. On {{dep_n}} metered SWITCH routers, February to October 2024, traffic explains under 1 % of power on {{dep_under1}} and at most {{dep_max}} on any. {{dep_big}}, the largest, stays within {{dep_p5}}–{{dep_p95}} W (5th–95th percentile) while its traffic reaches {{dep_gbps}} Gb/s (95th percentile). The IMC'25 study on the same network puts forwarding at about 6 W in total, 0.02 %.

Rejected: energy = traffic × kWh/GB. It would make a forecast of traffic a forecast of emissions, which the measurements contradict.

Two pools: access by allocated bandwidth, backbone by bytes

Equipment reserved for one party is billed to that party whatever it sends; shared transport is billed by use. Access routers go wholly to their site. Routers with both kinds of port are split first: the optics of customer-facing ports plus the chassis in proportion to customer-facing port capacity go to the access pool. In this window the access pool is {{kw_acc}} kW and the backbone pool {{kw_bb}} kW, about {{opt_share}} of it optical.

Rejected: one key for everything. Bytes alone bill a small site almost nothing for a router that exists only for it; capacity alone ignores who loads the shared trunks.

Bill every door, customers and external networks alike

A door is any port where traffic enters or leaves SWITCHlan: {{n_doors}} of them, grouped into {{n_parties}} parties. GÉANT, transit providers, exchanges and content networks get bills too. Each byte is billed half at the door where it enters and half where it leaves, so nothing is counted twice. With one rule for all doors, telling customers from external partners changes the name on a bill, not the numbers.

Rejected: billing customers only. Customers would then pay for the external half of every byte, and the ISPs and exchanges would get no statement.

Routers missing from the export become group doors

{{n_missing}} routers that still run are absent from the export. I don't see their ports, but I do see our cables into them. Each becomes one door that stands for everyone behind it, with the traffic on those cables as its volume. The three border routers ix3, ce1 and ba3 carry most external links, so most external partners appear as three groups.

Rejected: dropping them. Their traffic would vanish from the denominator and their energy from the inventory (routers {{mwh_nodns}} → {{mwh_dns}} MWh in {{year}} once they were counted).

Allocate emissions, not kilowatt-hours

Each pool is multiplied by the hourly factor of its site's zone before it is split. Every site is Swiss except CERN, which draws from the French grid. Liechtenstein sits in Swissgrid's control area.

Rejected: one national annual factor. In {{year}} the Swiss factor's hour-of-day average ranged about {{hod_ratio}}× ({{hod_min}}–{{hod_max}} g/kWh) and its monthly average about {{mon_ratio}}× ({{mon_min}}–{{mon_max}} g/kWh). The bill should show that.

Combustion-only, flow-traced grid factor

Scope 2 under the GHG Protocol counts combustion only; upstream and losses go to Scope 3. Flow tracing counts imports, which matters for Switzerland: its own generation is almost carbon-free, but winter imports are not. Lifecycle factors are reported separately.

Checked (October 2026) against my own series built from Energy-Charts generation and cross-border flows: within ±4 % in 2023–24 and hourly correlation about 0.96, but 28 % higher in 2025.

A day-ahead bill, issued once at midnight

The forecast bill for a day is made at 00:00 UTC, from information available then. Traffic comes from the block logs: one 24-hour path per link, issued at the start of the day. {{rule_sentence}} Energy needs no forecast.

Rejected: the rolling logs, which re-forecast every 5 minutes. A plan for tomorrow is made once; rolling forecasts would flatter the result.

Measure the bill error, and take it apart

The headline metric is daily WAPE: the sum of absolute errors of all parties' daily bills over the sum of the bills. I also replace one forecast at a time with the truth: actual grid with forecast traffic, and actual traffic with forecast grid. That shows which forecast matters.

5 · What it means

  • Fix the grid forecast first. It drives most of the day-ahead error. Electricity Maps serves a 72-hour forecast through the same API; it can't be tested on a past window, so the next step is to log it daily and score it as the days arrive. Weather-driven imports are what make the Swiss factor hard to predict.
  • Traffic forecasting matters most for the big parties. Small sites pay mostly for access, which is known in advance; their forecast bill is nearly exact once the grid factor is. The traffic model shows up in the bills of external networks and large customers.
  • Keep accounting and steering apart. The billed g/GB rewards sending when the network is busy and the grid is clean. That is a fair way to share a fixed footprint, but moving traffic does not reduce what the network emits. For green routing, the marginal signal is the right one, and inside SWITCHlan it is close to zero. The real levers are hardware: power supplies, optics, and switching off unused capacity.
  • A forward bill is still useful. Customers can plan with it, and SWITCH can publish both numbers per party and hour: the attributional bill that adds up to the inventory, and the marginal value that says what a change would actually save.

6 · Limits and open data

  • Missing routers. {{hidden_share}} of the window's bill goes to group doors behind routers missing from the export, including {{border_share}} to external networks behind ix3, ce1 and ba3. A request to SWITCH to re-export them is drafted.
  • Transit through hidden routers. ce1 and ez31 sit mid-path, so their group doors include bytes that only pass through. That overstates them.
  • Power carried forward from 2024. About {{modelled_share}} of router energy is modelled, not measured, and facility overhead is set by host type.
  • One season. The test window is spring and summer, when the Swiss grid is cleanest and steadiest. Winter imports will make the grid forecast harder.
  • Interface roles. DNS reflects today's configuration; a few ports may have been reassigned since 2025. Customer names come from site hosts and a handful of DNS labels, not from SWITCH's port descriptions.

7 · Reproduce

The pipeline is part of the repository: the packages green_routing.network and green_routing.carbon, and the recipe recipes/carbon. With the year run's dense logs in place and the reference inputs in $GR_REFERENCE_DIR:

  1. gr-network all → link ends, router tiers, interface roles, far ends from reverse DNS
  2. gr-carbon grid → hourly grid factors (--download fetches them; needs EM_API) and the day-ahead rules
  3. gr-carbon inventory → router models, power, service spans, optical shelves, site energy, Scope 2
  4. gr-carbon dependence → power against traffic for every metered router
  5. gr-carbon allocate → access and backbone pools, doors and parties
  6. gr-carbon bills → settled and forecast bills, errors, scheduling test
  7. recipes/carbon/build_report.py → this page; recipes/carbon/verify.py checks the headline numbers

recipes/carbon/run.sh all runs them in order. Method and data: docs/carbon.md.
