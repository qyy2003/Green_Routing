Green Routing · what a byte on SWITCHlan should cost in CO₂

SWITCHlan carbon rates

Bills are settled with the traffic that really happened. The open question is the rate: how many grams of CO₂ a gigabyte carries in a given hour. I compared two ways to set it on the same {{days}} days ({{window_text}}): one flat rate for every byte, or a rate that follows the equipment each byte crosses. For the flat rate I forecast the total traffic with one model and tried every model in the ladder. For the path rate I estimated who talks to whom from the counters and checked that method against real flow data from another research network.

Built {{built}} by recipes/carbon_rates. Backbone emissions only (the access part of a bill is the same in both plans). Location-based, combustion-only grid factors. A screening study.

In brief
  1. Flat rate. The backbone emitted {{bb_t}} t over {{tb_bytes}} PB carried, an average of {{flat_rate}} g/GB. Hour by hour it ranges {{rate_p10}}–{{rate_p90}} g/GB (10th–90th percentile), because emissions follow the grid and the rate divides them by the traffic.
  2. One model for the total traffic works. Trained on the total alone, {{best_model}} forecasts tomorrow's hourly traffic with {{best_wape}} error, against {{pers_wape}} for persistence. Summing the per-link models' forecasts for all 414 doors does slightly better ({{bu_wape}}), and so does a small GBDT trained on the hourly total ({{gbdt_wape}}).
  3. The grid factor decides the rate forecast. With the real grid factor the day-ahead rate is off by {{rate_err_a}}; with tomorrow's grid factor guessed, every model lands near {{rate_err_f}} hour by hour ({{rate_err_fd}} on daily averages). Perfect traffic would still leave {{rate_err_grid}}.
  4. Path rates split the network. Charging each byte for the routers and fibre it crosses, in proportion to use, moves {{usage_moved}} of the backbone emissions from one party to another. But backup links that carry almost nothing then bill their whole power to a handful of bytes: parties with {{usage_tiny_traffic}} of the traffic pay {{usage_tiny_share}} of the path bill.
  5. Charged by capacity instead, the path part nearly vanishes. SWITCHlan runs at about {{util}} of its capacity, weighted by emissions. If a byte pays only for the capacity it occupies, {{cap_share}} of the backbone emissions follow paths; the remaining {{cap_idle}} pay for idle headroom and are shared like the flat rate.
  6. Counters can stand in for flow data, where routing is known. On Abilene, where real NetFlow matrices exist, matrices estimated from counters give path bills within {{ab_em_total}} of the flow-based ones over {{ab_weeks}} weeks; the flat rate would be {{ab_flat}} off. On SWITCHlan the routing has to be inferred, and two plausible matrices still disagree by {{sw_sens}} on usage-priced bills.
  7. The second dataset tells the same story at 2004 scale. Rebuilt with its own equipment (Juniper T640 routers, leased Qwest wavelengths) and EPA's 2004 grid factors, Abilene's flat rate comes out at {{ab_flat_rate}} g/GB, about {{ab_vs_sw}} times SWITCHlan's: older routers, a coal-heavy grid ({{ab_gkwh}} g/kWh) and little traffic. One model forecasts its total with {{ab_best_wape}} error ({{ab_best_model}}); with a grid factor that does not change by the hour, all of the rate forecast's error ({{ab_best_rate}}) comes from the traffic.

1 · Two ways to set the rate

Both plans split the same backbone emissions: the backbone share of every router and the whole optical layer, each multiplied by its site's hourly grid factor. They differ only in who pays which gram.

Flat rate

Every byte carried in hour h pays the same.

rate(h) = B(h) / T(h) B = backbone emissions T = bytes carried, counted once

Needs one number per hour: the total traffic. Forecast it, and the rate follows.

Path rate

A byte pays the rate of each router and fibre span on its route.

rate(s→t, h) = Σ on the path E_r(h) / X_r(h) E_r = emissions of resource r X_r = its traffic (usage) or capacity

Needs the routes and, for the bills, who sends how much to whom.

2 · Plan A: one model for the total traffic

The total is the bytes SWITCHlan carries, counted once at the doors where traffic enters or leaves (customer ports, customer sites, links to other networks). Bytes in and bytes out agree to within {{cons}} over the window, so nothing is lost or counted twice. I wrote the total as a single 5-minute series ({{steps}} steps, July 2025 to July 2026) and ran the whole forecasting ladder on it, unchanged: the same split, calendar features and training budget as the 534-link benchmark.

The rate forecast is issued at 00:00 UTC for the next 24 hours, like a day-ahead price: the backbone emissions come from static power times the forecast grid factor (same hour yesterday), the traffic from the model.

3 · Plan B: a rate that follows the path

How it is built

  1. The network as a graph. {{routers}} routers and {{links}} directed links, from traffic matching and SWITCH's reverse DNS, with each link's hourly load from the counters. The {{hidden}} routers missing from the export are endpoints that stand for everything behind them.
  2. Routes. Shortest paths with equal-cost splitting, as OSPF does. SWITCH does not publish its link costs, so I let the counters choose: I hid a fifth of the links from the estimator and predicted their loads. Costs by link capacity predicted them best (table below), so the routes use those.
  3. Resources. Each router's backbone power, and each site's optical shelves spread over the fibre spans that end there. Routers carry {{router_share}} of the backbone power, fibre spans the rest.
  4. Who talks to whom. A traffic matrix for every hour, estimated from the counters: start from a gravity guess (big senders talk to big receivers) and adjust it until it reproduces the measured link loads and door totals, keeping every flow non-negative. {{unseen}} of the traffic enters through ports the export does not show; it is kept as a separate unseen flow and its cost is shared flat.
  5. Bills. Each resource's hourly emissions are split over the bytes that cross it; each flow's cost goes half to the door where it enters and half to the door where it leaves. The bills add up to exactly the same {{bb_t}} t as the flat plan.

Fit: error on the link loads the estimator saw. Hidden links: error predicting loads it did not see (mean of 3 random fifths). Gravity only: the same prediction without adjusting the guess.

Two ways to price a resource

By usage (emissions ÷ bytes that cross it) is the textbook path allocation. It makes rates swing with load: a backup fibre span carrying almost nothing charges its whole power to the few bytes on it. The worst span on SWITCHlan, {{worst_span}}, works out at about {{worst_span_rate}} g/GB in a typical hour, {{worst_span_x}} the flat rate.

By capacity (emissions ÷ capacity) charges a byte for the share of the equipment it occupies. What nobody uses, the idle headroom, is shared by all bytes like the flat rate. Rates are then stable and only reflect how much equipment, and how efficient, a route crosses.

4 · Flow data or counters?

SWITCHlan has no flow data we can read. The bucket holds SNMP counters, power, optics and CPU, nothing from NetFlow or IPFIX, and the research checkout is not on this machine. So the flow method was tested on a network where both exist: Abilene, the US research backbone, whose real traffic matrices from NetFlow are public (Zhang et al., 2004) together with its routing. I rebuilt the counters those matrices would produce (link loads and router totals), estimated the matrices back from the counters alone, and compared the path bills over {{ab_weeks}} weeks ({{ab_hours}} hours, {{ab_nodes}} routers), with Abilene's own equipment and grid factors (section 5).

Matrix error: distance from the real matrix (relative L2, median hour). Bill error: path bills per router against the flow-based ones, summed over the period; worst router in brackets.

Three things follow. Counters recover the path bills to within {{ab_em_total}} in total over {{ab_weeks}} weeks (the worst router is {{ab_em_max}} off), much closer than the {{ab_flat}} gap between flat and path. Priced by capacity, the matrix hardly matters at all ({{ab_cap}} error), because Abilene too ran mostly empty ({{ab_util}} utilisation). And Abilene is the easy case: its routes are known and its counters exact. On SWITCHlan the estimator misses hidden links by {{sw_holdout}} instead of {{ab_holdout}}, and swapping the estimated matrix for the plain gravity guess moves usage-priced bills by {{sw_sens}}, but capacity-priced bills by only {{sw_sens_cap}}.

5 · Second dataset: Abilene, with measured flows

Abilene was the US research backbone (Internet2). Its 12 routers in 11 cities were joined by 15 links, all leased OC-192 (10 Gbit/s) wavelengths on Qwest's network except one OC-48. The archive holds who-sent-what-to-whom every 5 minutes from NetFlow (1 in 100 packets, scaled back up), with the real OSPF routing. I used the {{ab_weeks}} contiguous weeks from {{ab_period}}: {{ab_gbps}} Gbit/s on average. One day is repeated in the archive (dropped), one day is missing, and one corrupt hour (70 Gbit/s through a 10 Gbit/s router) was removed. The same ladder of models, the same flat and path rates and the same bills ran on it unchanged, through the same code.

Electricity and CO₂ inputs

The archive has traffic only, so the energy had to be rebuilt from vendor documents, and the grid factors from EPA records for 2004.

That adds up to {{ab_it}} kW of equipment (routers {{ab_rt}} kW, optics {{ab_opt}} kW), {{ab_site}} kW at the sites with overhead ({{ab_site_lo}}–{{ab_site_hi}} kW across the low and high cases), and about {{ab_tyr}} t CO₂ a year at a power-weighted {{ab_gkwh}} g/kWh. eGRID gives one rate per region and year; hourly factors do not exist for 2004, so Abilene's rate varies only with its traffic.

6 · What I recommend

  • Publish the flat rate, forecast a day ahead with one model on the total. {{best_model}} or a small GBDT on the hourly total are both fine; the traffic part is solved to about 11–12 %. Spend the next effort on the grid forecast, which causes {{rate_err_grid}} of hourly error on its own.
  • Do not price paths by usage. It is the most "causal" on paper, but its rates jump with backup routing and bill idle equipment to whoever happens to cross it. It also needs a far better traffic matrix than SWITCHlan's counters give.
  • If path matters, price by capacity. It is stable, needs only counters and routes, and tells customers something true: about {{cap_idle}} of the backbone's emissions pay for capacity nobody is using at that moment. That is the honest message for green routing too. Moving bytes between paths changes almost nothing; switching off idle capacity does.
  • Ask SWITCH for flow aggregates. Door-to-door byte counts per hour (NetFlow, sampled is fine) would replace the estimated matrix, settle the routing question and split the groups behind the missing routers.

7 · Limits

  • Routing is inferred. Link costs by capacity fit best, but SWITCH's real metrics, MPLS paths or BGP choices may differ.
  • Missing routers are endpoints. Traffic that transits them is counted as leaving and re-entering, which inflates their groups in both plans.
  • Unseen ports. {{unseen}} of the traffic enters through interfaces the export misses; under the path plan its cost ({{unseen_cost}} of emissions) is shared flat.
  • Capacity of a router is the sum of its port speeds, not its forwarding limit; optical power is split over spans by capacity, not by the real fibre and amplifier layout.
  • Abilene's energy is rebuilt, not metered. Line-card configurations per PoP, the wavelengths sharing Qwest's amplifiers and the 2004 PUE are assumptions (ranges in the inputs table); its grid factors are annual.
  • One season: spring and summer 2026.
